Jade Zhao · Dr Alexis Peirce Caudell · Indiana University

Honesty and Authenticity at the Border

What Hong Kong Customs should require of the digital human XiaoHui

Prepared for programme owners at the Customs and Excise Department, the Hong Kong Generative AI Research and Development Center, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner for Personal Data, and the Digital Policy Office.

1. Say it is an AI

Before the first reply, on every channel. Not only behind an agree button.

2. Name who corrects it

A human unit that fixes mistakes and writes them back into the knowledge base.

3. Let people check the channel

A practical way to tell the real service from a copied face or a stickered code.

I recommend Choice A

This is what I was thinking, and it is not a travel essay. Spring 2026 I was in Madrid with a camera: streets, a grocery aisle, this breakfast tray, a bocadillo. I was also a traveller who wanted a straight answer about ordinary things I might buy or carry. XiaoHui is Hong Kong Customs, not Spain. What I could not separate from those days is that a fluent, friendly face gets trusted first. That face should say what it is.

Executive summary

XiaoHui is already live. It answers questions in Cantonese, Putonghua, and English with a digital-human face on the website, messaging apps, and at control points. Right now the public materials do not force the system to say up front that it is an AI, name a human unit that actually corrects mistakes and writes them back into the knowledge base, or give travellers an easy way to check they are talking to the real service.

Looking at ACM Code Sections 1.3 and 2.9 and the Hong Kong Computer Society’s honesty rules, I think Customs should make those three things mandatory. That is Choice A. There is a softer option: require the disclosure now and phase in the other two after a short trial (Choice B). I do not think it is strong enough. No one has reported a fake XiaoHui yet. The risk still feels real enough that we should treat it seriously.

Formal dilemma question

In light of ACM Code Sections 1.3 and 2.9, should Hong Kong Customs in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region require XiaoHui to disclose that it is an AI, name a human correcting unit, and let travellers verify the channel (honesty and accountability), or take a phased approach that requires disclosure immediately while leaving the correcting unit and channel check for a measured later stage (approachability during transition)?

I recommend Choice A.

System mechanics and global context

XiaoHui (小慧) started in December 2023 as a voice system at the airport, Heung Yuen Wai, the Hong Kong-Zhuhai-Macao Bridge port, West Kowloon Station, and the Customs website. In March 2025 it moved onto WeChat, WhatsApp, and Facebook. In March 2026 Customs launched the current version: their own retrieval-augmented knowledge store, a localised model from the Center, and the digital-human face (Government of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region 2023; Hong Kong Customs and Excise Department 2025, 2026). Reporting in February 2026 had already described the upgrade (Tang 2026). Hong Kong’s Basic Law means local rules apply first (People’s Republic of China 1990).

The system works the way most of these things do. Retrieval pulls the relevant passages. A generative model then turns them into fluent answers. Fluency is not the same as accuracy. The Digital Policy Office itself treats hallucination as something that just happens and puts the accuracy duty on the people who build and run the system (Digital Policy Office 2025). Their success-story page is more upbeat. It highlights the three languages and says the human-like face makes the interaction feel more approachable (Digital Policy Office 2026).

There is an official disclaimer. It says the answers are for general reference, accuracy is not guaranteed, this is not the place for complaints, people should not type in personal data, and the government accepts no legal liability (Hong Kong Customs and Excise Department, disclaimer page). That is useful. It is still not the same as a clear statement at the start of every conversation that the speaker is a machine, plus a named person who will fix wrong answers, plus a practical way for travellers to confirm they are on the official channel.

People at borders are used to dealing with human officers. A friendly digital face borrows some of that trust. Research on government chatbots shows that making them look human can raise perceived trust, but it can also backfire if people later feel misled (Liu et al. 2025). Lab work has even shown people complying with robots that give wrong instructions (Karli, Cao, and Huang 2023). At a customs counter the stakes are concrete.

The Hong Kong Computer Society Code of Ethics is the local professional standard. It tells members to be honest and trustworthy, not to mislead people about whether a system is suitable, and to take account of how the people affected by the work actually experience it (Hong Kong Computer Society 2015, A.1, A.4, B.9). The ACM Code sits next to it. Section 1.3 is about honesty on limitations. Section 2.9 is about systems that are actually secure and usable (Association for Computing Machinery 2018). Both apply here because the problem is a public system that can be mistaken for an officer and that can be copied.

Stakeholder landscape

Stakeholder Power Interest Exposure
Programme owners in Customs High A modern multilingual service that does not embarrass the department Reputational risk arrives late
The Center Medium A successful deployment of their model They can be blamed for errors they never see once the answers leave the lab
Frontline officers Medium operational Clear information and less time spent fixing the system’s mistakes High. They meet the confused travellers (Elish 2019)
Cross-boundary travellers Low Accurate advice that does not cost them time, money, or a missed flight Highest immediate risk
Privacy Commissioner and Digital Policy Office Oversight Human accountability and accurate systems Institutional credibility

The power imbalance is obvious. The people who design the face have the least day-to-day exposure. The people who use it or clean up after it have the most.

Spring 2026 I was a student in Madrid. The days on this site are the ones I photographed: streets, a grocery aisle, breakfast, a bocadillo. I was also a traveller who wanted a straight answer about what I could buy and carry. That experience sits in the low-power row of this table. The person who needs the answer has the least say in how the face is designed.

I also keep thinking about who the design quietly leaves out. It seems to assume a fairly confident traveller who can read Chinese or English and treats the friendly face as extra colour. That leaves minority-language residents, domestic workers helping family with parcels, older travellers, people with low digital confidence, and neurodivergent people who may take a fluent answer as final. Research suggests that human-like design only helps people check for errors when they already know errors are possible (Ni and Huang 2026). Hiding the machine makes that harder.

Ethical analysis

I used two frameworks.

From a deontological angle the duty feels clear (Kant [1785] 2018). If a public system is going to speak like a person, the user should be able to tell it is a machine before they rely on the answer, should know the answer might be wrong, and should be able to check they are on the official channel. That is basically what ACM 1.3 and 2.9 and the Hong Kong Computer Society honesty clauses require. The Confucian idea of rectifying names points in the same direction. Call the thing what it is (Confucius [ca. 500 BCE] 2003, 13.3). Choice A meets that duty. Choice B only partly does.

From a utilitarian angle the picture is messier (Mill [1863] 2001). The benefits of the current approach are real: 24-hour service in three languages, a less intimidating face for first-time visitors, and less pressure on officers. Those gains matter. The costs also matter. A wrong answer about a controlled item can mean real delay, a fine, or a missed connection. The Air Canada chatbot case in British Columbia is not Hong Kong law, but it is the closest public example of a court refusing to let an organisation treat its chatbot as a separate entity that somehow absolves it of responsibility (Moffatt v. Air Canada 2024). Until Customs publishes accuracy numbers, the safer calculation is to lower the cost of error and the cost of someone copying the face.

I can see the argument for a phased approach. Making everything mandatory at once might make the interface feel colder and push more first questions onto officers. A short measured period after disclosure would let them gather data. Still, the deontological floor feels more important here. Honesty and accountability should not wait while the face is already public.

Security risk

No one has publicly reported a fake XiaoHui. That does not mean the risk is imaginary.

Customs points people to the system through QR codes at control points and on messaging apps. Once the digital face becomes familiar, it becomes valuable to copy. Someone could circulate a similar image and handle, or put a sticker over a posted code. The official materials name the real website, but they do not give travellers a quick, practical way to confirm authenticity at the moment they scan.

I notice the face problem because of the camera. A picture can make a street or a meal feel trustworthy before you know anything else about it. XiaoHui’s face is built to do a stronger version of that. Once people recognise it, copying it is worth more.

We already know digital faces and voices can be copied convincingly. The 2024 Arup case in Hong Kong showed how far the technology had already come when a worker transferred around HK$200 million after a video call with deepfaked colleagues (Chen and Magramo 2024; Leng and Chan 2024). Research on voice systems keeps showing that speaker verification is attackable and that watermarks can be removed (Guo n.d.).

A fake does not have to be obvious. It can answer in the same languages, look roughly the same, and give helpful-sounding advice that quietly serves someone else’s interest, for example by telling a traveller not to declare something. Because there is still no publicly named unit responsible for correcting live errors and writing the fix back into the knowledge base, it becomes harder to reconstruct what happened. The same design choices that keep the face approachable also make a convincing imitation more worthwhile.

Governance and regulatory context

Hong Kong’s legal system is separate (People’s Republic of China 1990). Mainland rules on generative AI are background, not the binding rules here (Cyberspace Administration of China et al. 2023). Inside Hong Kong the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance sets out six Data Protection Principles. Principle 1 limits collection to what is adequate and not excessive. Principle 3 limits use to the original purpose unless there is proper consent. The organisation that controls the data remains the accountable data user. The Privacy Commissioner’s framework and the Digital Policy Office guideline both keep repeating that humans stay responsible for AI output (Office of the Privacy Commissioner for Personal Data 2024; Digital Policy Office 2025).

None of these instruments currently forces the three specific steps this brief is asking for. That is the gap. Hong Kong already has professional honesty rules, privacy accountability rules, and technical guidelines. What it does not have for XiaoHui is a published requirement that every first screen say “this is an AI,” a named unit that actually updates the knowledge base when something is wrong, or a simple authenticity check travellers can use. Choice A closes that gap without waiting for new legislation.

One more thing that stands out: there are still no public accuracy figures, complaint numbers, or independent evaluations of XiaoHui. The launch notices and the success story describe the product. The absence of performance data is itself a transparency problem.

Action plan

I recommend implementing Choice A.

In the first six months the engineers and the Center should put a clear disclosure before every first reply on every channel: this is an AI system, not an officer, and answers can be wrong. The existing disclaimer language should appear before the conversation starts, not only behind an agree button. There should also be an obvious way to reach a human. A short public note on what has been tested and where the system still fails would help.

Programme owners should name the correcting unit, preferably from existing frontline staff, and give that unit a ticket process that actually updates the knowledge base. The unit’s name should appear on the disclosure screen. They should also publish one practical authenticity check: the official web address, how to recognise real Customs QR codes, and what to do if a code looks wrong. Then they should track complaints, confirmed corrections, any spoof reports, and how often people still ask for an officer after seeing the disclosure.

A review that tests the disclosure and authenticity language with minority-language users and less digital-confident travellers would be useful.

Over the next one to three years the Digital Policy Office should write the three conditions into the generative AI guideline and into procurement language for any future government digital human. An annual public note of failure modes for systems that give compliance advice would also help. Programme owners should keep the digital-human face only if the six-month data shows it still adds value once people know the limits. If problems cluster around particular languages, control points, or topics, the system should be narrowed there rather than just given a prettier avatar.

Civil society can keep publishing short annual checks on whether the disclosure is still present, whether corrections actually change later answers, and whether the authenticity guidance is still easy to find.

Implementation timeline

  • 0 to 3 months. Mandatory disclosure before every conversation, and a named correcting unit in place.
  • 3 to 6 months. Authenticity guidance published. First round of complaint and correction numbers released.
  • Year 1. Six-month audit published. Decision on whether to keep the digital-human face.
  • Years 2 to 3. The three conditions written into Digital Policy Office guidance and procurement rules. Annual failure-mode notes required.

Choice B is still available. Customs could require the disclosure now and give itself a short measured period before making the correcting unit and authenticity check mandatory. I think that is second-best. Approachability matters. It should not come ahead of the honesty and accountability requirements that apply when a system can be mistaken for an officer or copied by someone with better technology. Put the floor in place first. Then measure how warm the face still feels.
